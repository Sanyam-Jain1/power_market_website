The 1pm / 10pm rule

Charge at one in the afternoon, sell at ten at night. How much of a battery's value does the simplest schedule capture? Half, on four and a half years of IEX prices, and it loses money every winter.

Every afternoon India's solar fleet pushes day-ahead prices down to their lowest of the day. Every evening, when the sun has gone and the lights come on, they climb back up, often to the ₹10,000/MWh cap. A battery earns its living on that gap. The simplest way to work it is a fixed rule: charge from 13:00, sell from 22:00, every day, no software.

We tested that rule on every delivery day from 1 May 2022 to 31 October 2026: 1,645 days of IEX day-ahead prices, block by block. The battery is 100 MW / 200 MWh with an 87% round trip, kept between 10% and 90% charge, paying ₹20/MWh exchange fees on each side and a ₹300 wear charge on every MWh it discharges. We compare it with the best schedule possible in hindsight (every price known in advance), with a fixed 11:00 / 18:00 rule, and with an optimiser that plans each day on the average of the last 7 days' prices, which uses no future information.

Half the value, and getting better

Over the whole period the 1pm / 10pm rule earned 50% of the hindsight best. The fixed 11:00 / 18:00 rule earned 61%. Planning on last week's prices earned 96%.

The rule is getting better. It captured 37% in 2022 and 72% in 2026 so far, close to the 11:00 / 18:00 rule's 74%. Over the last 12 months it earned ₹23.4 lakh per MW, against ₹38.4 lakh for the hindsight best and ₹37.0 lakh for the 7-day plan.

The charge window is right. The sell window used to be wrong.

Charging from 13:00 is close to ideal. The cheapest two hours of the day most often start at 12:00, and over the last 12 months 13:00–15:00 averaged ₹1,796/MWh against ₹1,763 for 11:00–13:00. The rule loses little on the buy side.

It lost on the sell side. Until 2026, the dearest two hours of the day most often started between 17:00 and 19:00. In 2025, 22:00–24:00 averaged ₹5,688/MWh against ₹7,123 for 18:00–20:00: ₹1,435 less on every MWh sold. In 2026 the gap closed. Late-night blocks now often clear at the cap: 61% of 22:00–24:00 blocks did in 2026, against 30% in 2025. So 22:00–24:00 averaged ₹7,654 against ₹7,678. In 2026 the most common start for the day's dearest two hours is midnight.

It loses money in winter

From April to August the rule earns 80–90% of the hindsight best. From November to February it earns nothing, or less: summed over all four winters, the rule lost money in November, December, January and February. In winter the evening peak comes earlier, around 18:00, and fades fast. Across the four winters, 22:00–24:00 averaged ₹3,323/MWh, barely above the ₹3,041 the battery paid at 13:00–15:00. After fees, wear and the 13% lost in the round trip, the trade is a loss.

The rule lost money on 26% of all days, and on 15% over the last 12 months. Its eight worst days against hindsight all fell in late December 2022 and January 2023, when it lost ₹3,600–5,800 per MW a day while the best schedule earned ₹12,000–14,000.

What it means

  • A fixed rule is a floor, not a strategy. The gap between 50% and 96% is about ₹16 lakh per MW a year on a 2-hour battery (₹17.0 against ₹32.7 lakh since May 2022). It costs nothing more than planning each day on last week's prices.
  • If you must run a fixed rule, change it with the season. The rule's summer and winter results are so different that a single schedule for the year is the main mistake.
  • Watch late-night prices. The rule improved in 2026 because the late-night market tightened, not because the rule got smarter. If late-night supply catches up, it will fall back.

Method: the backtester and its baseline specification are open. The rule charges evenly over 13:00–15:00 and discharges evenly over 22:00–24:00, settled at IEX's actual day-ahead clearing prices. Each day starts and ends at 10% charge, so days are independent. Figures are after exchange fees and wear, before transmission charges and O&M. Backtested results are not a guarantee of future returns.
